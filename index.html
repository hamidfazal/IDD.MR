Primary Care Management of Intellectual Disability

A practical clinical guide to assessment, functional formulation, medical and psychiatric comorbidity, challenging behavior, caregiver support, safeguarding and longitudinal care in resource-limited settings.

Core clinical principle

Do not ask only, “What is the diagnosis?”

Also ask:

  • How does this person function?
  • What has changed from baseline?
  • Could pain or physical illness explain the change?
  • What support does the person and caregiver need?

1. Core Framework & Model Shift

Intellectual disability (ID) is a neurodevelopmental condition involving significant limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive functioning, with onset during the developmental period.

The model shift: move away from an IQ-only or “deficit” model toward a function + support + participation model.

Function

What can the person actually understand, communicate and do in everyday life?

Support

What level of assistance is needed for self-care, safety, decisions and community participation?

Participation

How can health care help the person participate in family, education, work and community life?

What primary care should aim to achieve

  • Maximize health, independence, communication and quality of life.
  • Identify and treat preventable medical and psychiatric problems.
  • Recognize behavioral change as a possible expression of pain, distress or unmet need.
  • Support caregivers and reduce avoidable caregiver burden.
  • Provide appropriate referral when specialist assessment is required.
WHO's mhGAP approach is designed to support evidence-based assessment and management in non-specialized health-care settings, particularly where specialist resources are limited.

2. Terminology & Important Distinctions

The historical term “mental retardation” is outdated and stigmatizing. Prefer intellectual disability (ID). The term intellectual and developmental disability (IDD) can be used when discussing the wider developmental context.

ID ≠ Mental Illness

A person can have ID without a psychiatric disorder, or can have both ID and depression, anxiety, psychosis or bipolar disorder.

ID ≠ Autism

Autism and ID are distinct conditions, although they may coexist.

ID ≠ Specific Learning Disorder

Difficulty with reading, writing or mathematics alone does not establish global intellectual and adaptive impairment.

ID ≠ Dementia

ID begins during development. Dementia involves acquired decline from a previous level of functioning.

3. Diagnostic Framework

Think in three domains rather than starting with an IQ number.

DomainWhat it means clinicallyPrimary-care questions
Intellectual functioning Reasoning, learning, problem solving, planning, judgment and learning from experience. How does the person learn? Can they solve simple problems? Understand consequences?
Adaptive functioning Ability to manage everyday conceptual, social and practical demands. Can the person communicate, manage hygiene, money, medication, safety and relationships?
Developmental onset The difficulties were present during the developmental period. When were developmental delays first noticed? What was the school/developmental history?
Clinical caution: an informal clinical impression is not equivalent to formal psychometric assessment. In primary care, the goal may be to identify likely ID, characterize support needs, identify comorbidity and arrange appropriate referral.

4. Functional Assessment

The most useful primary-care question is often: “What can this person do in real life, and what support do they require?”

Adaptive domainAssessExamples
Conceptual Communication, literacy, numeracy, money, time, planning. Understanding instructions, reading simple labels, counting money, understanding time.
Social Relationships, social judgment, boundaries, reciprocity, vulnerability. Recognizing unsafe situations, seeking help, understanding personal boundaries.
Practical Self-care, domestic skills, mobility, safety, work. Bathing, dressing, toileting, cooking, medication use, road safety, simple employment.

Support needs: a practical interpretation

Milder support needs

May perform many self-care and community tasks with targeted or intermittent support.

Moderate support needs

Often requires regular guidance for complex daily activities and decision-making.

Extensive support needs

May require substantial or pervasive assistance with communication, self-care and safety.

Important: support needs are influenced by communication, motor disability, epilepsy, sensory impairment, autism, family resources, environment and learned skills—not simply by a numerical IQ.

5. Clinical History

Developmental history

  • Pregnancy and birth complications
  • Prematurity/neonatal illness
  • Developmental milestones
  • Language and motor development
  • Previous assessments and school history

Current functioning

  • Communication style
  • Self-care and domestic skills
  • Mobility and falls
  • Education/work/vocation
  • Community independence

Medical history

  • Seizures/epilepsy
  • Vision and hearing
  • Dental disease
  • Constipation/reflux
  • Sleep problems
  • Medication effects

Psychiatric history

  • Change in mood or sleep
  • Anxiety or trauma symptoms
  • Psychotic symptoms
  • Mania/hypomania
  • Self-injury or aggression

Social and caregiver history

  • Who provides daily care?
  • Has there been a caregiver change, bereavement, relocation or family conflict?
  • Is the caregiver exhausted or unsupported?
  • Are there financial or transport barriers?
  • Could neglect, abuse, coercion or exploitation be present?

6. Physical Assessment: Look for Treatable Causes

A new behavioral change may be the patient's way of communicating pain or illness. Physical assessment should therefore be part of the initial response.

Oral & dental

Caries, gingivitis, abscess, chewing difficulty.

GI / bowel

Constipation, abdominal pain, reflux, altered bowel habits.

Neurological

Seizures, postictal states, focal signs, gait change, movement disorder.

Sensory

Hearing and vision impairment can present as withdrawal or behavior change.

Medication

New drugs, dose changes, sedation, akathisia, extrapyramidal symptoms or behavioral adverse effects.

Sleep

Sleep deprivation, fragmented sleep, snoring, possible sleep-disordered breathing.

Red flag: sudden regression, delirium, severe self-injury, unexplained injury, new neurological deficit, prolonged seizure, severe infection or immediate danger to self/others requires urgent assessment.

8. Challenging Behaviour: Behavior Is Communication

“Challenging behavior” is a description of behavior that creates risk, distress or major functional difficulty. It is not itself an explanation of why the behavior occurs.

Before asking “Which medicine should I use?”, ask “What function is this behavior serving?”

ABC Functional Assessment

A — AntecedentB — BehaviorC — Consequence
What happened immediately before? What exactly did the person do? What happened immediately afterward?
Demand? transition? noise? pain? denied item? social interaction? Describe observable behavior, frequency, duration and intensity. Did the demand stop? Did the person receive attention, escape, an item or sensory relief?

Common functional hypotheses

Escape

Avoidance of a difficult demand or unpleasant environment.

Attention

Access to social interaction or reassurance.

Tangible

Access to food, objects, activities or preferred items.

Sensory

Seeking or avoiding particular sensory experiences.

Communication

Expressing a need when verbal communication is limited.

Pain / distress

Expression of illness, discomfort, fear or emotional distress.

Stepwise behavioral management

  1. Define the behavior objectively.
  2. Identify patterns and triggers with an ABC log.
  3. Rule out pain, illness, medication effects, sleep problems and environmental stress.
  4. Modify the antecedent: simplify instructions, reduce noise, provide transition warnings and offer choices.
  5. Teach an alternative communication response.
  6. Reinforce desired behavior immediately and consistently.
  7. Measure whether frequency, intensity or duration changes.
  8. Refer when risk persists or the functional formulation remains unclear.

9. Communication: Make the Consultation Accessible

Speak simply

Use short, concrete sentences and one instruction at a time.

Allow processing time

Do not assume lack of response means lack of understanding.

Use visual support

Pictures, objects, gestures and demonstrations can improve understanding.

Check understanding

Ask the person to demonstrate or explain rather than simply asking “Do you understand?”

Always speak directly to the patient. The caregiver can provide essential collateral information, but the person with ID should remain the central participant in the consultation as far as possible.

10. Psychiatric Comorbidity

Psychiatric disorders can coexist with ID. The diagnostic challenge is often that conventional symptom descriptions are difficult to obtain.

Think “change from baseline”

  • New social withdrawal
  • Loss of previously enjoyed activities
  • Sleep or appetite change
  • Reduced self-care
  • New irritability or agitation
  • New anxiety or fearfulness
  • New unusual beliefs, perceptual experiences or marked behavioral disorganization
Depression in a person with limited communication

Look for a meaningful change in activity, sleep, appetite, social engagement, affect, self-care and previously rewarding activities. Use simple concrete questions and reliable collateral history while remembering that the caregiver's interpretation is not automatically the diagnosis.

Anxiety / trauma

Consider new avoidance, fearfulness, hyperarousal, sleep disturbance, startle, somatic complaints or behavioral changes after a stressful event.

Psychosis / bipolar disorder

Look for a clear departure from baseline: sustained changes in sleep, activity, speech, thought content, behavior and functioning. Specialist assessment is often helpful when diagnostic uncertainty is substantial.

Do not diagnose “behavioral problem” simply because the patient has ID. Establish the baseline and investigate the reason for the change.

11. Management Principles

Person-centred management

  • Set practical goals with the person and caregiver.
  • Prioritize communication, independence, participation and safety.
  • Use the least restrictive intervention that is effective.
  • Coordinate medical, psychological, behavioral, rehabilitation and social support where available.

Medication: a disciplined approach

Medication should treat a defined target or diagnosed comorbidity. It should not routinely substitute for environmental adaptation, communication support or caregiver training.
  1. Define the target symptom or disorder.
  2. Document baseline severity/frequency.
  3. Choose medication based on the actual diagnosis, comorbidity, interactions and risk profile.
  4. Start cautiously when medication is indicated and titrate according to response and tolerability.
  5. Monitor sedation, falls, extrapyramidal symptoms, weight/metabolic effects, orthostasis, cognition and behavioral worsening as relevant.
  6. Set a review date and reassess whether ongoing medication remains necessary.
The medication section is intentionally principle-based. Specific drug selection and dosing should follow the diagnosed condition, patient factors, local formulary, national guidance and specialist recommendations when appropriate.

12. Epilepsy & Neurological Comorbidity

Epilepsy is an important comorbidity in people with ID. Ask about seizure type, frequency, duration, injuries, triggers, adherence, rescue plans and medication adverse effects.

Clinical issueWhy it matters
Seizure classificationAntiseizure treatment should be matched to the seizure disorder.
Medication adherenceMissed doses may lead to breakthrough seizures.
Behavioral adverse effectsSome antiseizure medicines can contribute to irritability or behavioral change in susceptible patients.
CognitionSome medications may worsen cognitive/language functioning in susceptible patients.
Reproductive safetySome antiseizure medications have important pregnancy/reproductive safety considerations.
Do not choose an antiseizure medication merely because it may also influence mood. Seizure type, efficacy, interactions, adverse effects and individual patient factors come first.

When behavior changes in a patient with epilepsy

  • Ask whether seizures have increased.
  • Consider postictal behavioral change.
  • Review recent medication initiation, withdrawal or dose changes.
  • Look for sleep deprivation and other seizure precipitants.
  • Consider neurological referral for uncontrolled or diagnostically uncertain seizures.

13. Capacity, Consent & Autonomy

Intellectual disability does not automatically mean lack of decision-making capacity.

Capacity should be considered for the specific decision at hand. Support the person to understand and participate as much as possible.

  • Speak directly to the person.
  • Adapt information to their communication level.
  • Use supported decision-making techniques.
  • Allow adequate time.
  • Follow applicable local legal and institutional requirements when capacity is impaired.

14. Safeguarding

People with ID may be particularly vulnerable to abuse, coercion, neglect, exploitation and financial harm.

Physical

Unexplained injuries, bruising, recurrent injuries or fear of a caregiver.

Sexual

Unexplained genital injury, sexually transmitted infection, sexualized behavior or disclosure.

Financial / exploitation

Unexplained loss of money, possessions, benefits or coercive control.

Clinical response

  1. Ensure immediate safety.
  2. Where safe and appropriate, speak privately with the patient.
  3. Use simple, non-leading questions.
  4. Document objective findings and the patient's own words accurately.
  5. Follow local safeguarding, medico-legal and emergency procedures.
Immediate danger, severe injury or suspected ongoing abuse requires urgent action through the appropriate local pathway.

15. Caregiver Support

Caregiver education is not an optional add-on—it is often part of the treatment plan.

Teach practical skills

Demonstrate one skill, let the caregiver practise, then provide feedback.

Predictable routines

Regular eating, sleeping, learning, activity and medication routines can reduce avoidable distress.

Know the person

Identify strengths, preferences, stressors, triggers and the person's best way of learning.

Protect caregiver wellbeing

Ask about exhaustion, sleep, support, respite and practical barriers.

A useful caregiver question is: “What usually makes things better, and what usually makes things worse?”

16. Preventive & General Medical Care

Oral health

Dental review, hygiene and treatment of pain.

Vision / hearing

Assess sensory impairment that can affect function and behavior.

Nutrition

Assess undernutrition, obesity, dietary quality and physical activity.

Sleep

Screen for sleep problems and sleep-disordered breathing where appropriate.

Vaccination

Maintain routine immunization according to local recommendations.

Medication review

Reconcile medicines and assess adverse effects.

Cardiometabolic health

Monitor relevant risk factors, especially with psychotropic treatment.

Sexual/reproductive health

Provide accessible education and appropriate preventive care.

Screening

Provide age- and risk-appropriate preventive screening according to local guidance.

18. One-Page Primary-Care Clinical Workflow

Establish baseline
Define current problem
Medical + HELP review
Functional / psychiatric assessment
Person-centred plan
  1. Baseline: developmental history + current adaptive functioning.
  2. Presenting problem: health, behavior, communication, mood, seizures or regression.
  3. If behavior changed: use HELP—Health, Environment, Living situation, Psychiatric.
  4. Assess function: conceptual + social + practical domains.
  5. Check comorbidity: epilepsy, sensory impairment, dental/GI problems, sleep, nutrition and psychiatric illness.
  6. Build the plan: communication + environment + caregiver skills + appropriate medical treatment.
  7. Medication: clear indication + target + monitoring + review date.
  8. Safety: capacity, consent and safeguarding when relevant.
  9. Refer: when complexity or risk exceeds primary-care capacity.
  10. Follow up: measure change and simplify the plan where possible.

19. Case-Based Learning

Case 1 — Sudden aggression

Scenario: A 24-year-old man with ID who is normally calm begins hitting caregivers and refusing meals. The family requests an antipsychotic.

Clinical thinking: Do not jump directly to medication. Review dental pain, constipation, reflux, urinary symptoms, infection, sleep, medication changes and environmental stressors. Then use an ABC log to identify triggers.

Lesson: New aggression is a symptom requiring explanation—not automatically a psychiatric diagnosis.

Case 2 — “Won't cooperate” with examination

A woman with moderate ID becomes distressed during clinic examinations.

Use predictable sequencing, visual explanation, short instructions, caregiver coaching and gradual exposure rather than automatically escalating force.

Lesson: communication difficulty and fear may be driving the behavior.

Case 3 — Depression or baseline behavior?

A man with ID becomes socially withdrawn, sleeps more, eats less and stops attending his usual activity.

Ask what changed from baseline, obtain appropriate collateral history, investigate medical causes and assess for depression.

Lesson: do not dismiss new symptoms as “part of the disability.”

20. Quick Self-Test

Question 1: A person with ID suddenly becomes aggressive. What should be considered first?

Answer: B. Sudden behavioral change requires assessment for physical illness, pain, medication effects, environmental disruption, safeguarding and psychiatric causes.

Question 2: Which is the most useful way to assess functional severity?

Answer: B. Functional assessment considers conceptual, social and practical adaptive functioning and the support required.

Question 3: In an ABC log, what does “A” represent?

Answer: B. Antecedent = what happened immediately before the behavior.

Question 4: Does ID automatically mean lack of decision-making capacity?

Answer: No. Capacity is decision-specific. The person should be supported to participate as much as possible.
